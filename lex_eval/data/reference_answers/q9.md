## 1. Question

> Explain the concepts of legal professional privilege, common interest privileges and limited waiver and how they all apply in the context of sharing legal advice between the UK and Scottish Governments in light of the Freedom of Information (Scotland) Act 2002 and the Environmental Information Regulations 2004

Legislation and case law are both in scope.

**Scope of this answer:** Covers what legal professional privilege, common interest privilege and limited waiver are, and how each bears on sharing legal advice between the UK and Scottish Governments when that information is requested. Because the same document is held on both sides, it covers both regimes named or implied by the question: the Freedom of Information (Scotland) Act 2002 and the Environmental Information (Scotland) Regulations 2004, and the Freedom of Information Act 2000 and the Environmental Information Regulations 2004. The UK instruments are covered only so far as they bear on privileged legal advice.

### 1. Summary Answer (BLUF)

Three distinct ideas are in play, and the most important thing to get right is that **the second and third are questions of Scots law that English authority does not settle either way**.

1. **Legal professional privilege (in Scots terms, confidentiality of communications).** This is what [section 36(1) of FOISA](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/asp/2002/13/section/36) imports: information "in respect of which a claim to confidentiality of communications could be maintained in legal proceedings" is exempt. Note the wording. FOISA does not say "legal professional privilege"; it uses the Scots formulation.
2. **Common interest privilege.** Well established in **English** law: parties with a sufficient common interest in a matter can exchange privileged material without losing privilege against the outside world. Because section 36(1) asks what could be maintained **in legal proceedings**, and those are Scottish proceedings, the English authority is **persuasive in Scotland rather than binding**, and how far it is followed is a question of Scots law. In [R (Prudential plc) v Special Commissioner of Income Tax [2013] UKSC 1](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2013/1), Lord Reed recorded that the law in this area "developed separately in Scotland from in England" and that although the general principle is shared, "there are a number of differences in the case law in relation to particular aspects of the law" ([103]). That is a reason to **check** the Scots position, not a reason to assume the doctrine is unavailable.
3. **Limited waiver.** The proposition that disclosing privileged material for a specific, limited purpose waives privilege only for that purpose. The same point applies: the doctrine is familiar in English law, and its Scottish counterpart has to be established as a matter of Scots law rather than assumed either way.

**Where the Scots answer is found, and why it is not here.** The sources that settle these two questions are decisions and guidance of the Scottish Information Commissioner on section 36, and Court of Session authority on confidentiality and waiver. Neither is reachable through the tools used for this answer: Find Case Law does not index the Court of Session, and the Commissioner's material is not legislation. **The absence of a Scottish authority below is therefore an absence of coverage, not evidence that the doctrines do not apply**, and a reviewer should read section 2.6 with that in mind.

**The practical consequence for sharing advice between the two governments.** Do not transplant an English textbook proposition into a FOISA answer without checking the Scots position, and do not dismiss it either. What section 36(1) actually asks is whether a claim to confidentiality of communications could be maintained in legal proceedings. The course that works whichever way the doctrinal question is answered is to build the protection into the disclosure itself: share on expressly confidential terms, with identified recipients, for an identified purpose, and record those terms.

**One regime is not enough.** The question names both FOISA and the UK Environmental Information Regulations 2004, and that pairing is the point. Advice shared between the two governments is held by both, so it is requestable from each, under **four** instruments in two regimes: FOISA and the Environmental Information (Scotland) Regulations 2004 in Scotland, the Freedom of Information Act 2000 and the Environmental Information Regulations 2004 at UK level. They are close but not identical, and section 2.7 sets out where they differ.

**And two structural points that are often missed.** The privilege exemption is **qualified** on both sides, subject to the public interest test: FOISA section 36(1) is not in the absolute list in section 2(2), and FOIA section 42 is not in the absolute list in section 2(3). And if the information is **environmental**, the freedom of information Act is the wrong instrument on either side of the border: the request falls to be answered under the relevant environmental information regulations, where there is no privilege exception at all.

### 2. Detailed Analysis

#### 2.1 What section 36 says, and what it does not say

> **Section 36 - Confidentiality**
> (1) Information in respect of which a claim to **confidentiality of communications** could be maintained in legal proceedings is exempt information.
> (2) Information is exempt information if—
> (a) it was obtained by a Scottish public authority from another person (including another such authority); and
> (b) its disclosure by the authority so obtaining it to the public (otherwise than under this Act) would constitute a breach of confidence actionable by that person or any other person.

([asp/2002/13, s.36](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/asp/2002/13/section/36))

Section 36(1) does not create a privilege. It borrows one. The test is whether a claim **could be maintained in legal proceedings**, so the content of the exemption is whatever the substantive law of privilege says. That is why the doctrinal question in this request matters: if a doctrine is not part of Scots law, it cannot support a section 36(1) claim.

The drafting is deliberately Scottish. Lord Reed noted in *Prudential* that "legal professional privilege" was adopted in preference to the older term "confidentiality", which "could lead to confusion between the privilege and the different sense in which 'confidentiality' is employed in other contexts". The same judgment quotes the modern statutory formula used when a provision has to work in both systems, in Schedule 36 to the Finance Act 2008: information is privileged if a claim "to legal professional privilege, or (in Scotland) to confidentiality of communications as between client and professional legal adviser, could be maintained in legal proceedings". FOISA uses only the Scottish half of that formula.

#### 2.2 Section 36(1) is qualified, not absolute

> **Section 2 - Effect of exemptions**
> (1) To information which is exempt information by virtue of any provision of Part 2, section 1 applies only to the extent that—
> (a) the provision does not confer absolute exemption; and
> (b) in all the circumstances of the case, the public interest in disclosing the information is not outweighed by that in maintaining the exemption.
> (2) For the purposes of paragraph (a) of subsection (1), the following provisions of Part 2 (and no others) are to be regarded as conferring absolute exemption— (a) section 25; (b) section 26; **(c) section 36(2)**; (d) section 37; and (e) [parts of] section 38 ...

([asp/2002/13, s.2](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/asp/2002/13/section/2))

**Section 36(2) is listed; section 36(1) is not.** So the privilege limb is subject to the public interest test in section 2(1)(b), and establishing that material is privileged is only the first half of the answer. This is a common error: privilege is close to absolute as a matter of the general law, but the FOISA exemption built on it is not.

Section 29(1)(c) is worth holding alongside section 36(1). Information held by the Scottish Administration relating to "the provision of advice by any of the Law Officers or any request for the provision of such advice" is exempt information, and that exemption is also qualified ([s.29](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/asp/2002/13/section/29)). For advice from or to the Law Officers, both exemptions are likely to be engaged.

#### 2.3 If the information is environmental, FOISA is the wrong instrument

> **Section 39(2)** Information is exempt information if a Scottish public authority—
> (a) is obliged by regulations under section 62 to make it available to the public in accordance with the regulations; or
> (b) would be so obliged but for any exemption contained in the regulations.

([asp/2002/13, s.39](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/asp/2002/13/section/39))

The regulations under section 62 are the Environmental Information (Scotland) Regulations 2004 ([ssi/2004/520](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ssi/2004/520)). Environmental information is therefore routed out of FOISA and into the EIRs, where the framework is materially less favourable to withholding.

#### 2.4 The environmental route

> **Regulation 10 - Exceptions from duty to make environmental information available**
> (1) A Scottish public authority may refuse a request ... if— (a) there is an exception to disclosure under paragraphs (4) or (5); and (b) in all the circumstances, the public interest in making the information available is outweighed by that in maintaining the exception.
> (2) In considering the application of the exceptions ... a Scottish public authority shall— (a) **interpret those paragraphs in a restrictive way**; and (b) **apply a presumption in favour of disclosure**.
> (4) ... (e) the request involves making available **internal communications**.
> (5) ... to the extent that its disclosure would, or would be likely to, **prejudice substantially**— ... (b) **the course of justice**, the ability of a person to receive a fair trial ...; (d) **the confidentiality of the proceedings of any public authority where such confidentiality is provided for by law** ...
> (6) To the extent that the environmental information ... relates to information on emissions, a Scottish public authority shall not be entitled to refuse to make it available under an exception referred to in paragraph (5)(d) to (g).

([ssi/2004/520, reg.10](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ssi/2004/520/regulation/10))

Four differences from FOISA follow, and each makes withholding harder:

- **There is no privilege exception as such.** Legally privileged advice has to be brought within regulation 10(5)(d), confidentiality of proceedings provided for by law, or regulation 10(5)(b), the course of justice, or regulation 10(4)(e) if it is genuinely an internal communication.
- **A substantial prejudice threshold applies** to the regulation 10(5) exceptions. It is not enough that the material is privileged; disclosure must be likely substantially to prejudice the protected interest.
- **Regulation 10(2) requires restrictive interpretation and a presumption in favour of disclosure.** There is no equivalent instruction in FOISA.
- **Regulation 10(6) removes the (5)(d) to (g) exceptions entirely for information on emissions.** Privileged advice about an emissions matter may be disclosable however clearly privileged it is.

One point of interaction is worth flagging for the shared-advice scenario. Regulation 10(4)(e) protects **internal** communications. Advice exchanged between the UK Government and the Scottish Government is, on any natural reading, not internal to either. That is a reason for caution about sharing environmental legal advice specifically.

#### 2.5 Legal professional privilege: what is common to both systems

*Prudential* is the most useful retrievable authority. The Supreme Court there declined to extend legal advice privilege beyond communications with lawyers to advice from accountants. Two points travel:

- The privilege is treated as fundamental. The judgment records *R (Morgan Grenfell) v Special Commissioner* [2002] UKHL 21, where a statute could only remove "a fundamental human right" if it "expressly stated" it was doing so or the intention "appear[ed] by necessary implication".
- **Legal advice privilege is confined, in broad terms, to communications between a solicitor and client**; litigation privilege (in Scotland, *post litem motam* confidentiality) covers material prepared in contemplation of litigation, which may include material from non-lawyers.

Lord Reed then addressed the Scottish position expressly, at [102] to [105]. The passage repays reading in full, but the operative sentences are:

> "No question arises in this appeal as to the scope of the privilege in Scots law ... The law in this area developed separately in Scotland from in England. The two systems have however developed in the same direction. **There are a number of differences in the case law in relation to particular aspects of the law**, but the general principle, its fundamental importance, and the considerations of public policy which underlie it, are common to both systems."

That is authority for the framing of this answer. The **core** of privilege is shared. Particular **aspects** are not necessarily shared, and each has to be checked against Scots law rather than assumed from English textbooks.

#### 2.6 Common interest privilege and limited waiver: a Scots law question, unresolved here

**Common interest privilege**, as understood in England and Wales, allows a privileged document to be passed to another party with a sufficient common interest in its subject matter without waiving privilege as against the world. **Limited waiver** is the related proposition that disclosure for a specific, limited purpose waives privilege only for that purpose.

Both matter to this question because both, if available, are the answer to "does privilege survive sharing the advice with the other administration?".

Neither could be **resolved** from the sources available for this answer, and it is important to be precise about why:

- Searches of Find Case Law for both doctrines returned recent judgments matching the words but not the leading authorities, which pre-date the database or were not surfaced by a search that returns results in date order rather than by relevance.
- A search restricted to the Inner House of the Court of Session was **rejected: the Court of Session is not an available court in this database at all**. Its own coverage note records that "the Scottish Court of Session (CSOH/CSIH) and Sheriff Courts are NOT indexed" and that "for Scottish matters, this database contains only UK Supreme Court and Privy Council decisions".
- The Scottish Information Commissioner's decisions and guidance under section 36, which are the most directly relevant body of material on how the exemption treats shared and limited-purpose disclosure, are not legislation and were not reachable either.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions, which is the honest position:

- **The English cases are persuasive, not binding.** *Prudential* confirms the two systems developed separately and differ in particular respects, so English common interest privilege authority is a strong starting point for a Scottish public authority's FOISA position but not a conclusive one, and an answer should say which it is relying on.
- **Equally, nothing here says they are unavailable.** Nothing retrieved says that Scots law lacks an equivalent protection for common interest sharing or limited-purpose disclosure, and the sources that would settle it were out of reach. A conclusion that a Scottish authority loses confidentiality by sharing advice on limited terms would need Scots authority of its own, just as much as the opposite conclusion would.

**This is the single point in this answer most in need of a Scots lawyer's confirmation.** For a reviewer, the useful question is not "is the doctrine English?" but "what does the Commissioner's guidance and the Court of Session say about whether confidentiality of communications survives a limited-purpose or common-interest disclosure?".

#### 2.7 The UK side: the same document, a different rulebook

The question names the UK Environmental Information Regulations 2004, and the UK regime has to be in the answer because a document shared between the two governments is held on both sides.

**Freedom of Information Act 2000, section 42.**

> **Section 42 - Legal professional privilege**
> (1) Information in respect of which a claim to **legal professional privilege or, in Scotland, to confidentiality of communications** could be maintained in legal proceedings is exempt information.

([ukpga/2000/36, s.42](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/2000/36/section/42))

Note the drafting difference. FOIA section 42 carries **both** limbs of the formula, so a UK department holding advice given under Scots law can still claim the exemption; FOISA section 36(1) carries only the Scottish limb. Section 42 is **not** among the absolute exemptions listed in section 2(3), so it is qualified and subject to the public interest test in the same way as FOISA section 36(1) ([s.2](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/2000/36/section/2)).

**Section 39** routes environmental information out of FOIA, as section 39(2) of FOISA does for FOISA, and section 39(1A) defines the regulations by reference to the power in section 74 ([s.39](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/2000/36/section/39)).

**Environmental Information Regulations 2004, regulation 12.** The structure mirrors the Scottish regulation 10, but two differences matter for withholding privileged advice:

> **Regulation 12**
> (1) ... a public authority may refuse to disclose environmental information requested if - (a) an exception to disclosure applies under paragraphs (4) or (5); and (b) in all the circumstances of the case, the public interest in maintaining the exception outweighs the public interest in disclosing the information.
> (2) A public authority shall **apply a presumption in favour of disclosure**.
> (4) ... (e) the request involves the disclosure of **internal communications**.
> (5) ... to the extent that its disclosure would **adversely affect** - ... (b) **the course of justice** ...; (d) **the confidentiality of the proceedings** of that or any other public authority where such confidentiality is provided by law ...
> (9) To the extent that the environmental information to be disclosed relates to **information on emissions**, a public authority shall not be entitled to refuse to disclose that information under an exception referred to in paragraphs (5)(d) to (g).

([uksi/2004/3391, reg.12](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/uksi/2004/3391/regulation/12))

- **The UK threshold is lower.** Regulation 12(5) asks only whether disclosure "would **adversely affect**" the interest. The Scottish regulation 10(5) requires substantial prejudice. On the same facts, the UK exception is easier to engage than its Scottish counterpart.
- **The UK regulations do not command restrictive interpretation.** Regulation 12(2) requires a presumption in favour of disclosure, but there is no UK equivalent of the Scottish regulation 10(2)(a) instruction to interpret the exceptions restrictively.
- **Emissions are treated the same way.** Regulation 12(9) matches regulation 10(6): for information on emissions the (5)(d) to (g) exceptions are unavailable on both sides.

The practical upshot for shared advice is that **the two administrations can lawfully reach different answers on the same document**, and that on environmental information the Scottish authority is working to the tighter test. Neither can bind the other, and a release by one destroys the confidentiality the other is relying on, so the sharing arrangement should say who answers what.

#### 2.8 Applying this to advice shared between the two governments

- **Two regimes, two answers.** The same document held by both administrations is answerable by the Scottish Government under FOISA and the EI(S)R, and by the UK department under FOIA 2000 and the EIR 2004. A decision to withhold by one does not bind the other, and as section 2.7 shows the tests are not identical.
- **Section 36(1) turns on whether privilege survives the sharing.** That is the question the doctrines above are usually deployed to answer. Where their availability in Scots law is uncertain, the protection should be built into the disclosure itself: share on expressly confidential and limited terms, to identified recipients, for an identified purpose, and record it.
- **Section 36(2) may add a second line.** Where one administration obtains information from the other, section 36(2) exempts it if disclosure would be an actionable breach of confidence, and that limb **is** absolute under section 2(2)(c). It depends on the law of confidence rather than on privilege, so it does not stand or fall with the doctrinal questions above.
- **The public interest test still has to be worked.** For section 36(1) under FOISA and for every exception under regulation 10, exemption is the beginning of the analysis rather than the end.

### 6.1 Research plan

1. **Retrieve the FOISA exemption and its public interest gateway**
   Retrieve the confidentiality exemption in FOISA and the provision governing the effect of exemptions, and establish whether the privilege limb is absolute or qualified.

2. **Retrieve the environmental information route**
   Retrieve the exceptions regulation of the Environmental Information (Scotland) Regulations 2004 and identify which exceptions can carry privileged legal advice, together with the interpretive rules that constrain them.

3. **Retrieve the UK side of both regimes**
   Retrieve the legal professional privilege exemption, the absolute-exemption list and the environmental information provision of the Freedom of Information Act 2000, and the exceptions regulation of the Environmental Information Regulations 2004, so the cross-government answer rests on both rulebooks rather than one.

4. **Establish the law of privilege that the exemption imports**
   Search Find Case Law for Supreme Court authority on the nature and scope of legal professional privilege, including any statement of the Scottish position, since the FOISA exemption is framed by reference to what could be maintained in legal proceedings.
