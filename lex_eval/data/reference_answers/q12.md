### 1. Summary Answer (BLUF)

The case is **[Graham Andrew Evans v R [2025] EWCA Crim 1150](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2025/1150)** (Court of Appeal, Criminal Division; Holroyde LJ (Vice-President), May J and Wall J; judgment 5 September 2025). **The appeal against conviction was dismissed.**

The appellant was an **operational support grade ("OSG")** at HMP Hewell, convicted of **misconduct in a public office**. Working alone overnight in a prison block, he failed to carry out 24 of the 38 checks required by a prisoner's Assessment, Care in Custody and Teamwork ("ACCT") plan, and falsified the log to show that he had. The prisoner, Mr Olgun, was found hanging and died a week later. A gross negligence manslaughter count was withdrawn from the jury at half time because causation could not be proved.

**The point of the case is what makes someone a "public officer".** It is not the job title or the formal grade. It is the **actual duties and responsibilities the individual held at the material time**, and whether performing them properly discharges a responsibility of government in which the public has a significant interest going beyond the interest of anyone directly affected. On that test an OSG, alone overnight and responsible for a block of prisoners, was a public officer, even though an OSG has no power of arrest and, save in emergencies, no power to search a prisoner, use force or enter a cell.

### 2. Detailed Analysis

#### 2.1 The three grounds of appeal

1. That the judge was wrong to rule there was a case to answer, because an OSG is not a public officer.
2. That, the jury having been unable to be sure that any breach caused the death, no jury could find the breach serious enough to amount to the offence.
3. That the judge should not have directed the jury that they could consider the likely consequences of the breach.

All three failed.

#### 2.2 The test for public officer status

The court took the elements of the offence as settled, summarising them (at [21]) as:

> "(1) A public officer acting as such (2) wilfully neglects to perform his duty and/or wilfully misconducts himself (3) to such a degree as to amount to an abuse of the public's trust in the office holder (4) without reasonable excuse or justification."

On who is a public officer, the court adopted the three questions from **R v Mitchell [2014] EWCA Crim 318** at [16], which it described (at [37]) as "a clear and helpful indication of the approach to be taken":

> "First, what is the position held? Second, what is the nature of the duties undertaken by the employee or officer in that position? Third, does the fulfilment of those duties represent the fulfilment of one of the responsibilities of government such that the public have a significant interest in the discharge of that duty which is additional to or beyond an interest in anyone who might be directly affected by a serious failure in the performance of that duty?"

Two features of that approach carry the decision. First, as *Mitchell* emphasised at [17] and this court repeated at [25], **the focus is on the duties and responsibilities of the individual accused, not on the overall responsibility of the employing organisation**. Second, as **R v Cosford [2013] EWCA Crim 466** held at [34], any "strict confinement" of the offence is directed not at the position held but at **"the nature of the duty undertaken"**.

The court also noted (at [37]) that **the categories of activity which may be held to be a public office are not closed**.

#### 2.3 Applying the test to an OSG

The first two *Mitchell* questions were not in dispute. On the third, the court held (at [39]) that proper performance of the appellant's duties

> "would represent the fulfilment of the responsibility of government for the safety and security of persons whom the state had deprived of their liberty."

The reasoning is specific to what this appellant was actually doing that night. He was the **one person present in the block** who held a position of responsibility towards all the prisoners in it, the one person immediately able to assist if a prisoner's health or safety was compromised, and the one person immediately able to ensure compliance with the ACCT plans. The court found it "impossible to regard the appellant's duties and responsibilities as being no more than three discrete duties of care owed to three individual prisoners".

It followed (at [41]) that the judge was "plainly correct" to rule that a reasonable jury could be sure the appellant was a public officer acting as such at the material time.

This is why the defence argument from powers failed. Counsel had argued that an OSG has no more power than a civilian secretary in a prison, and that the offence should not expand simply because the prison authorities found it expedient to give auxiliary staff more functions. That argument addresses the grade; the test addresses the duties.

#### 2.4 Consequences and causation

Grounds 2 and 3 rested on the acquittal on manslaughter. The court accepted the prosecution's position that **misconduct in public office is a conduct offence**: there is no requirement to prove that the misconduct caused harm. That the jury could not be sure the breach caused the death therefore did not prevent them finding the breach serious enough to amount to an abuse of the public's trust, and the judge was entitled to direct the jury that they could take the potential consequences of the conduct into account when judging its seriousness.

#### 2.5 What a summary of this case should not say

The reported outcome is easy to state and easy to state shallowly. A summary that gives only the facts, the offence and the dismissal has missed the reason the case is worth reading: it is authority on **how public officer status is decided**, and it decides it by reference to what the person was actually responsible for, not what they were called.

### 3. Jurisdiction & Status

- **Jurisdiction.** England and Wales. Misconduct in a public office is a common law offence of the law of England and Wales; it has no direct Scots law equivalent.
- **Status.** A judgment of the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division), binding on the Crown Court and below.
- **Naming.** The question calls the case "Rex v Evans (Graham)". Find Case Law reports it as "Graham Andrew Evans v R", the appellant being named first on appeal. Both refer to the same judgment.
- **Limit of this answer.** Written from the judgment text alone. *Mitchell* and *Cosford* are described as they are quoted and applied in this judgment; neither was retrieved separately.
