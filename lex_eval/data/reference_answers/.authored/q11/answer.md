### 1. Summary Answer (BLUF)

**There is no single statutory definition of "occupier" to borrow.** The term takes its meaning from the enactment it appears in, read with that Act's purpose and its other provisions. Where a notice-giving power leaves it undefined, the common law test of **control** is the usual starting point rather than an automatic answer.

The three points that matter for choosing a recipient:

1. **The occupiers' liability statutes deliberately do not define the term.** Section 1(2) of the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 says in terms that its rules "shall not alter the rules of the common law as to the persons on whom a duty is so imposed". The Occupiers' Liability Act 1984 then defines "occupier" by pointing back to the 1957 Act. The chain ends at the common law.
2. **Scotland has a statutory formulation; England and Wales does not.** Section 1(1) of the Occupiers' Liability (Scotland) Act 1960 defines an "occupier of premises" as "a person occupying or having control of land or other premises". The same words, "occupying or having control of", appear in section 5(1) of the 1957 Act.
3. **The common law test is sufficient control, and it admits of more than one occupier.** *Wheat v E Lacon & Co Ltd* [1966] AC 552 is the leading authority: whoever has a sufficient degree of control over premises is an occupier, exclusive occupation is not required, and control need not be complete.

For a notice, the practical consequence is that **"the occupier" is unlikely to be settled by title or residence alone**, and that control of the relevant premises for the relevant purpose is usually the better guide. How far the *Wheat* test carries across is a question about the particular Act: it was decided about occupiers' liability, and a notice power with a different purpose may point somewhere narrower or wider. Who must be served is then determined by that Act's own service provisions.

### 2. Detailed Analysis

#### 2.1 The 1957 Act does not define who an occupier is, and says so

> **Section 1 - Preliminary**
> (1) The rules enacted by the two next following sections shall have effect, in place of the rules of the common law, to regulate the duty which an occupier of premises owes to his visitors ...
> (2) The rules so enacted shall regulate the nature of the duty imposed by law **in consequence of a person's occupation or control of premises** ... **but they shall not alter the rules of the common law as to the persons on whom a duty is so imposed or to whom it is owed** ...

([ukpga/Eliz2/5-6/31, s.1](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/Eliz2/5-6/31/section/1))

This is the single most useful provision for the question asked. It is an express statement that Parliament codified **the content of the duty** and left **the identity of the duty holder** to the common law. Section 2 then imposes the common duty of care on "an occupier of premises" without saying who that is ([s.2](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/Eliz2/5-6/31/section/2)).

The nearest the Act comes to a formulation is section 5(1), which speaks of "a person occupying or having control of the premises" ([s.5](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/Eliz2/5-6/31/section/5)).

#### 2.2 The 1984 Act defines the term by cross-reference

> **Section 1(2)** For the purposes of this section, the persons who are to be treated respectively as an occupier of any premises ... are —
> (a) **any person who owes in relation to the premises the duty referred to in section 2 of the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957** (the common duty of care) ...

([ukpga/1984/3, s.1](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/1984/3/section/1))

So the 1984 Act's occupier is the 1957 Act's occupier, and the 1957 Act's occupier is the common law's occupier. No statutory definition is reached by following this chain, which is itself the answer to the question "where is the definition?".

#### 2.3 Scotland: a statutory formulation of the same idea

> **Section 1 - Variation of rules of common law as to duty of care owed by occupiers**
> (1) The provisions of the next following section of this Act shall have effect, in place of the rules of the common law, for the purpose of determining the care which **a person occupying or having control of land or other premises (in this Act referred to as an "occupier of premises")** is required, by reason of such occupation or control, to show towards persons entering on the premises ...

([ukpga/Eliz2/8-9/30, s.1](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/Eliz2/8-9/30/section/1))

This is a usable synthesised definition: **occupying or having control of** land or other premises. The Act extends to Scotland only (s.5(1), [ukpga/Eliz2/8-9/30, s.5](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/Eliz2/8-9/30/section/5)).

Section 3 is worth noting alongside it. Where premises are occupied under a tenancy and the landlord is responsible for maintenance or repair, the landlord owes the same care in respect of dangers arising from a failure to carry out that responsibility ([s.3](http://www.legislation.gov.uk/id/ukpga/Eliz2/8-9/30/section/3)). That is a duty imposed on someone who is not the occupier, which is a caution against reading "occupier" as a synonym for "the person responsible for the premises".

#### 2.4 The common law test: sufficient control

*Wheat v E Lacon & Co Ltd* [1966] AC 552 remains the leading authority. It is not in the Find Case Law database, which does not extend back to 1966, but its terms are set out in modern judgments that apply it.

In [Kanwarjit Singh Juj v John Lewis Partnership Plc [2023] EWCA Civ 1507](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2023/1507) at [20], the Court of Appeal recorded Lord Denning's formulation:

> "Wherever a person has a **sufficient degree of control** over premises that he ought to realise that any failure on his part to use care may result in injury to a person coming lawfully there, then he is an 'occupier' and the person coming lawfully there is his 'visitor' ..."

In [William Brown v Morgan Sindall Construction and Infrastructure Ltd [2025] EWHC 2204 (KB)](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/kb/2025/2204) at [195], the High Court drew out the two features that matter most here:

> "There is authority that the basis for an occupier's liability is 'occupational control, i.e., control associated with and arising from presence in and use of or activity in the premises'. See Wheat v Lacon [1966] AC per Lord Pearson at 552 (HL). The same authority supports the proposition that **that control does not need to be complete**. See per Lord Denning in the same case: '... **In order to be an "occupier" it is not necessary for a person to have entire control over the premises. He need not have exclusive occupation. Suffice it that he has some degree of control.**' (Page 578)."

Three things follow, and they are what an adviser needs:

- **Control, not title.** Ownership, tenancy and residence are evidence of control, not substitutes for it.
- **Control need not be exclusive or complete.** A degree of control suffices.
- **There can therefore be more than one occupier of the same premises at the same time**, each in respect of the aspects they control.

Lord Pearson's phrase is the useful one for a notice: control "associated with and arising from presence in and use of or activity in the premises".

#### 2.5 Applying this to serving a notice on "the occupier"

Where the enabling provision does not define the term, the meaning is taken from that enactment and its purpose. The common law test above is a starting point and an analogy, not a definition that displaces the Act. Four practical steps follow from the material above:

1. **Read the enabling Act as a whole first.** An interpretation section elsewhere in the Act, or a general service-of-notices provision, may define or qualify the term even though the notice power itself does not. The purpose of the particular notice also colours the meaning: a notice about the physical condition of premises points to whoever controls their state, while one about an activity points to whoever controls that activity.
2. **Ask who has control of the relevant premises for the relevant purpose**, using *Wheat v Lacon* as the analogy: presence, use and activity, not title. Check that a control-based reading actually fits what this notice power is for before adopting it.
3. **Do not assume a single answer.** Where control is divided, for example between a landlord who retains common parts and a tenant who controls the let unit, both may be occupiers for different purposes.
4. **Take service from the enabling Act, not from the definition.** Whether a notice may be addressed to "the occupier" at the premises, and whether more than one person can or must be served, is answered by that Act's service provisions and any applicable general rule, not by the number of people who might satisfy a control test.

### 3. Jurisdiction & Status

- **England and Wales.** Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 and Occupiers' Liability Act 1984. Neither defines "occupier" independently of the common law.
- **Scotland.** Occupiers' Liability (Scotland) Act 1960, which extends to Scotland only and does contain the formulation "occupying or having control of land or other premises".
- **Status of the case law.** *Juj* is a Court of Appeal decision and *Brown* a first instance decision of the King's Bench Division; both are cited here as accurate statements of *Wheat v Lacon*, which is a decision of the House of Lords and remains the governing authority.
- **Limit of this answer.** *Wheat v E Lacon & Co Ltd* [1966] AC 552 itself could not be retrieved: Find Case Law does not cover 1966, so the authority is reached through modern judgments quoting it. The quoted passages should be checked against the report before the definition is relied on in advice.
- **Limit of this answer.** Confined to the meaning of "occupier". It does not address the separate statutory rules on how a notice must be served, or on whom service is deemed effective.
